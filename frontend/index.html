A risk analysis project, explained in plain English

Real market data. Real company filings. Real risk models.

Imagine you own $10 million of stock in 30 big US companies. This project answers three questions about it: how much could you lose on a bad day, what would a real market crash do to it, and which of those companies are in the best financial shape?

Scroll

The question

How much could we lose — and how would we know if our estimate is wrong?

Banks estimate their risk every single day. The most common estimate is a single number: “on 99 days out of 100, we shouldn't lose more than this much.” That's a promise, and promises can be checked. So this project checks it against what actually happened, day after day, for four years.

But an ordinary bad day isn't a crisis. So we also replay real disasters — 2008, 2020, 2022 — to see what they would have done to the portfolio. Finally, we give 25 of the companies a financial health score, using the numbers from their official annual reports.

Words you'll see

VaR (Value-at-Risk)
The loss we don't expect to exceed on 99 days out of 100 — a daily “risk limit”.
Expected Shortfall
When a really bad day does come, how much we lose on average.
Breach
A day when the real loss was bigger than the risk limit — the model got that day wrong.
Backtest
Checking a model against what really happened, using only information it would have had at the time.
Stress test
“What if a crash like 2008 happened to us?” — replaying a real crisis on today's portfolio.
10-K
The official annual financial report every US public company files with the SEC, the market regulator.

01 — Daily risk

How bad can a normal day get?

  1. 1 · Set the limit

    Every day, we look back over the past year and ask: what's the biggest loss we'd expect on 99 days out of 100? That number is the day's risk limit — the white line. It rises after turbulent periods and falls when markets calm down.

  2. 2 · Check what happened

    Then we look at what really happened the next day. If the loss was bigger than the limit, it's a breach — a red dot. The model got that day wrong, and it stays on the record.

  3. 3 · Grade the model

    A 99% limit should be broken about once every 100 days. Far more breaches means the model underestimates risk; far fewer means it's too cautious. A standard statistical test tells us whether the difference is just bad luck.

02 — Crises

What would a real crash do?

The daily risk limit only learns from the past year, so it can't imagine a crisis it hasn't seen. Stress testing fills the gap: we take the portfolio and replay what each of its stocks actually did during real market crashes.

03 — Company health

Which companies are on solid ground?

We read the official annual reports of 25 companies and turned a few key numbers into a health score from 0 to 100 — higher is stronger — then grouped them into Tier 1 (strongest) to Tier 5 (weakest).

What goes into the score

    04 — Limitations

    Here's where this breaks.

    The health score is not a credit rating.

    Agencies like S&P and Moody's study the business, its debts and its plans, and debate the result in committee. This score only reads a handful of numbers off the annual report — and its link to real-world risk is weak.

    Thirty stocks aren't the whole market.

    It's a fixed list of big US companies, owned in equal amounts. There are no bonds, currencies or anything else in it — so an interest-rate shock only reaches it indirectly, through how the stocks react.

    Four years isn't much to judge a model.

    At 99%, we only expect about ten breaches in the whole test. With numbers that small, it's hard to tell a good model from a lucky one.

    We only picked survivors.

    Every company here still exists in 2026. Replaying 2008 with companies we already know made it through makes that crisis look gentler than it really was.

    Today's patterns don't last forever.

    The 1994 estimate uses how these stocks reacted to interest rates in 2021–2026. Thirty years earlier, the same stocks may have behaved very differently.

    Some comparisons are rough.

    Comparing a one-day limit with a months-long crash assumes each day is unrelated to the last. In a crisis, bad days come in a row — so treat those comparisons as a rough guide only.
